# Deja Swing: Measuring Batter Adaptation Through Swing Mechanics

A hitter can make a meaningful adjustment without producing an immediate change in the box score, which makes adaptability difficult to observe and easy to confuse with short-run performance. This paper introduces a framework that treats adaptability as a latent response measured through swing mechanics. Using public MLB pitch-level and bat-tracking data, a historical model estimates the swing expected for the pitch, count, location, trajectory, handedness, pitcher, and player context. The difference between expected and realized attack angle, attack direction, swing-path tilt, bat speed, and swing length defines a multivariate mechanical deviation. Exposure then accumulates across every earlier pitch of the same type seen by the hitter within a game, regardless of pitcher. Across two seasons, later same-type looks are associated with smaller adjusted deviations in the combined measure and in each component after accounting for other pitch exposure and plate-appearance progress; the multivariate association also persists when same-pitcher repetition is entered separately. A hierarchical measurement model converts these noisy responses into hitter-specific adaptability curves with explicit uncertainty. Independent temporal tests, however, do not support stable player rankings, and a chronological pitch-type-conditioned experiment finds no clear improvement in later contact prediction from earlier same-type mechanics beyond pitch context and prior outcomes. The paper therefore establishes a reproducible measurement framework and a replicated population pattern while defining the evidence still required to validate adaptability as a persistent, decision-relevant player skill.
